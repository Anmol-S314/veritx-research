The proof

Case studies — measured, not asserted.

Every case study here is reproducible from a committed toolchain: seeded experiments that run bit-identically, known-answer gates with exact answers, and results that were wrong before they were right. Client versions ship redacted; these public ones are our own pipeline.

1. The honest 5.4×

A KV-multicast architecture claimed a 5.4× throughput win. On the surface it was right. Under the discipline of derating, it became honest:

  • 269 tok/s → 244 tok/s once DRAM efficiency was measured instead of assumed (a starved row buffer was caught by a known-answer gate before it confirmed a lie with a lie).
  • The 5.4× survives — but only at its correct operating point. Longer contexts must shard KV across dies, and that is a fabric question with its own measured crossover (the "keep KV off the fabric" law).
  • Lesson for clients: the headline wasn't false; it was unqualified. Finding the envelope of a true claim is most of an audit.

Known-answer gate: a memory-access law with an exact answer, g−1 = 7, pinned before measurement — then reproduced. The same gate caught a bug in our own multicast patch, twice. This is the standard we hold every re-measured number to.

2. Burstiness, not bandwidth, starves control

An 8×8 mesh carrying two traffic classes: DMA bursts to 8 diagonal NICs (class 0) and 1-flit control packets (class 1). Every cell below moves the same DMA flit load — 0.08 flits/cycle/node across 64 nodes — only the burst length changes.

 Control latency in cycles. Isolated control plane: 33.2 flat. 1 VC: 1.36× → 6.68× vs the plane as bursts grow. 4 VCs: 1.24× worst-case — VCs segregate the classes.

Doubling DMA bandwidth is harmless; doubling DMA burst length quintuples control starvation. A 1-flit control packet waits for the whole burst to drain the shared VC. The fix is not a fancier topology — it's plane separation: virtual-channel planes (or express lanes: cmesh k=4 c=4, vc=4, express ON 23.1→35.8 cyc vs OFF 26.3→67.4 cyc, never worse at any cell). Only a physically separate plane is flat by construction.

Why this is an audit case study: the claim being checked was "topology matters at the periphery." Half-true — the word that was wrong was topology. What actually matters is plane count, boundary bandwidth, and protocol handling at the edge. The shape verdict survives contact with a peripheral-rich chip, and the lever moved from "which fabric" to "how the traffic classes meet the fabric." That kind of correction is what an audit is for.

3. Validating a deadlock-freedom claim

Deadlock-freedom proofs are a special case of the same problem: a claim that must survive an adversary, not a workload. Our worked audit takes a deadlock-freedom claim (textbook and practical), runs it through the gate discipline — topology-routing deadlock vs protocol-level deadlock, injection as a stress axis, formal model-checking (CBMC/SymbiYosys) as a cross-check rather than a stamp — and publishes the method as a template.

What clients take away: a repeatable procedure for checking a freedom claim, plus the distinction between what a proof establishes and what it doesn't. The template is public; the client's claim stays redacted.
